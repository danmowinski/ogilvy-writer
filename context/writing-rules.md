# Writing rules

The standard every brief, draft, and critique in this project is held to.

Sources: Confessions of an Ad Man/Ogilvy; Ogilvy on Advertising/Ogilvy; The Unpublished Ogilvy.

## General Rules

1. "What you say is more important than how you say it." The substance and central promise of an advertisement influence purchasing more than its presentation.

2. "Unless your campaign is built around a great idea, it will flop." A campaign needs a powerful central idea to distinguish it and achieve exceptional results.

3. "Give the facts." Consumers want useful information, so advertisements should provide concrete facts rather than empty slogans and adjectives.

4. "You cannot bore people into buying." Advertising must be distinctive and interesting enough to earn attention.

5. "Be well-mannered, but don’t clown." Advertising should be friendly and respectful without becoming frivolous, comic or excessively cute.

6. "Make your advertising contemporary." Advertising should reflect the lives, attitudes and psychology of current consumers.

7. "Never write an advertisement which you wouldn’t want your own family to read." Advertising should be truthful and ethical.

8. "Don’t be a copy-cat." Distinctive campaigns should arise from original thinking rather than imitation.

9. These rules are strong guidelines, not a checklist to be satisfied. They frequently conflict with one another, and no advertisement can obey all of them at once. When they conflict, the writing must make sense first: follow the rules that serve the advertisement and set the others aside. Do so deliberately, and be able to say which rule you ignored and why.

## Headlines

1. "The headline is the 'ticket on the meat. Use it to flag down the readers who are prospects for the kind of product you are advertising." The headline should immediately identify the people most likely to buy the product.

2. "Every headline should appeal to the reader’s self-interest. It should promise her a benefit..." The headline should clearly state what the reader stands to gain.

3. "Always try to inject news into your headlines." The headline should present something new about the product, its use or its development.

4. "Other words and phrases which work wonders are HOW TO, SUDDENLY, NOW, ANNOUNCING, INTRODUCING, IT’S HERE, JUST ARRIVED, IMPORTANT DEVELOPMENT, IMPROVEMENT, AMAZING, SENSATIONAL, REMARKABLE, REVOLUTIONARY, STARTLING, MIRACLE, MAGIC, OFFER, QUICK, EASY, WANTED, CHALLENGE, ADVICE TO, THE TRUTH ABOUT, COMPARE, BARGAIN, HURRY, LAST CHANCE." Use proven words and phrases that attract attention and encourage action.

5. "Five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy, so it is important that these glancers should at least be told what brand is being advertised." That is why you should always include the brand name in your headlines. Include the brand name so readers know what is being advertised even when they read nothing else.

6. "Include your selling promise in your headline." This requires long headlines." Put the principal product benefit in the headline even when doing so makes it longer.

7. "People are more likely to read your body copy if your headline arouses their curiosity; so you should end your headline with a lure to read on." Give readers a compelling reason to continue into the body copy.

8. "Some copywriters write tricky headlines—puns, literary allusions, and other obscurities. This is a sin." Avoid clever or obscure language that readers must work to understand.

9. "Research shows that it is dangerous to use negatives in headlines." Avoid negative constructions because readers may overlook the negative word and misunderstand the claim.

10. "Avoid blind headlines—the kind which mean nothing unless you read the body copy underneath them; most people don’t." Make the headline meaningful without requiring readers to consult the body copy.

## Body copy

1. "Don’t beat about the bush—go straight to the point." State the message directly rather than approaching it through indirect or complicated comparisons.

2. "Avoid superlatives, generalizations, and platitudes. Be specific and factual." Use precise and engaging facts rather than vague, exaggerated or commonplace claims.

3. "You should always include testimonials in your copy." Use credible endorsements to make the product’s claims more believable.

4. "Another profitable gambit is to give the reader helpful advice, or service." Provide useful information that benefits the reader as well as promoting the product.

5. "I have never admired the belles lettres school of advertising." Avoid elaborate literary writing that draws attention away from the product.

6. "Avoid bombast." Do not use inflated, grandiose or self-important language.

7. "Unless you have some special reason to be solemn and pretentious, write your copy in the colloquial language which your customers use in everyday conversation." Write in the natural, everyday language used by the intended customer.

8. "Resist the temptation to write the kind of copy which wins awards." Prioritise sales results over recognition from advertising-industry judges.

9. "Good copywriters have always resisted the temptation to entertain." Concentrate on selling the product rather than merely amusing the audience.
